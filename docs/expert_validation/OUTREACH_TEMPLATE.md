# Outreach Templates

Drafts for contacting subject experts about an exploratory look at the prerequisite graphs.

**Guidance for using these.** The ask is one opinion from someone who teaches the subject. It is
not an invitation to join a study, and nothing in these messages should suggest otherwise. Keep
the first message short; a long message reads as a large request. Fill every square bracket, and
delete anything you cannot say truthfully. Do not describe anyone as a collaborator, adviser or
participant unless they have said they are.

Replace: `[Name]`, `[subject]`, `[institution]`, `[specific thing you have read or seen]`.
Sender: Ritayush Suchismita Dey, subhrajd@gmail.com.

---

## 1. Academic email, first contact

Subject line options:

- Quick question about prerequisite structure in [subject]
- Student research project: would you glance at a [subject] prerequisite map?

> Dear Professor [Name],
>
> I am a school student working on a computational research project about STEM curriculum
> structure, and I am writing to ask whether you would be willing to give an informal opinion on
> part of it.
>
> The project represents each subject as a graph of concepts, where an arrow from one concept to
> another says that the first should normally be studied before the second. I built the
> [subject] graph myself from textbooks and syllabi, which means the prerequisite claims in it
> have no expert backing at all. That is the gap I am trying to close.
>
> The specific ask is small: I have a one-page sheet listing the [N] [subject] concepts and the
> prerequisite relationships between them, ordered so that the ones my software depends on most
> come first. If you could look at the top ten and tell me whether any of them strike you as
> wrong, backwards, or missing something, that would be genuinely useful. Ten minutes of your
> time, in whatever form is easiest, including a few lines of reply.
>
> I came across your work on [specific thing you have read or seen], which is why I thought of
> writing to you.
>
> To be clear about what this is: it is a student project, not affiliated with a university, and
> it has not been tested with real students. I am not asking you to endorse it or to commit to
> anything further. If you would rather not, or if this is not a good time, I completely
> understand.
>
> I can send the sheet as a single attachment, or a link, whichever you prefer.
>
> Thank you for reading this far.
>
> Best regards,
> Ritayush Suchismita Dey
> subhrajd@gmail.com

**Notes.** `[N]` is 35 for mathematics, 16 for computer science, 15 for physics, and 14 each for
biology and chemistry. Only claim familiarity with someone's work if you have actually read it;
a vague compliment is worse than no compliment.

## 2. Academic email, shorter variant

For a less formal context, or where a long message is unlikely to be read.

> Dear Dr [Name],
>
> I am a school student building a computational model of STEM curriculum structure, where each
> subject is a graph of concepts linked by prerequisite relationships. I wrote the [subject]
> prerequisites myself, so they currently have no expert check behind them.
>
> Would you be willing to look at ten of them and say whether any look wrong or backwards? It is
> a one-page sheet and should take about ten minutes. No commitment beyond that, and an informal
> reply is fine.
>
> Happy to send the sheet if you are interested.
>
> Thank you,
> Ritayush Suchismita Dey

## 3. LinkedIn connection note

LinkedIn limits connection notes to roughly 300 characters, so this is deliberately bare.

> Hi [Name], I am a school student working on a computational project mapping prerequisite
> structure in [subject]. I am looking for an informal expert opinion on whether my prerequisite
> relationships are sensible. Would you be open to a short question? No commitment either way.

Follow-up message once the connection is accepted:

> Thank you for connecting. The project represents [subject] as a graph of concepts, with arrows
> meaning "study this before that". I built the [subject] relationships from textbooks and
> syllabi, so they have no expert check behind them yet.
>
> The ask is small: a one-page sheet with the concepts and the ten most important prerequisite
> relationships, and your view on whether any of them are wrong, backwards, or missing a step.
> Ten minutes or so, informal reply entirely fine.
>
> It is a student project with no institutional affiliation, and it has not been tested with real
> students. Happy to send the sheet if you are interested, and no problem at all if not.

## 4. Polite follow-up

Send **once**, no earlier than two weeks after the first message. If there is no reply to the
follow-up, do not send a third.

> Dear Professor [Name],
>
> I wrote a couple of weeks ago about my student project on prerequisite structure in [subject],
> asking whether you might give an informal opinion on ten prerequisite relationships.
>
> I know this is a busy time, so I am not expecting a reply; I only wanted to make sure the
> message had not been buried. If you would prefer not to, a one-line no is a perfectly good
> answer and I will not write again.
>
> Thank you either way.
>
> Best regards,
> Ritayush Suchismita Dey

## 7. Things not to write

- Do not call the project "validated", "peer-reviewed" or "evidence-based". None of those is true.
- Do not say or imply that other experts have already agreed to take part. Until someone has, the
  honest number is zero.
- Do not claim the system improves learning outcomes. No learning outcome has been measured.
- Do not describe an informal opinion as participation in a study.
- Do not attach the full 119-relationship set to a first email. Send one subject sheet.
- Do not set a deadline for a favour.
- Do not use an institutional or school affiliation the project does not have.

---

## 8. Tracking

Keep a private record outside the repository: name, institution, subject, date contacted, channel,
date of follow-up, outcome. It prevents accidental duplicate contact and keeps the "contacted once,
followed up once" rule enforceable.
